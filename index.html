About This Calculator

Learn · Rotor balancing

Use the calculator, and understand what it is doing

Every number on this page comes from a few ideas you can learn in ten minutes. Open the topics below in order, or jump to what you need. Each one ends with how it appears in this workbench.

1×vibration at running speed is what we balance
αinfluence coefficient: how the machine reacts to 1 g
−O/αthe correction weight, in one line
1Why balance at all?Unbalance, force and the 1× signal

No rotor is perfect. If its mass centre sits a small distance e off the axis of rotation, the rotor carries an unbalance U = m · e (g·mm). Spinning turns that into a rotating force:

F = m · e · ω²

The force grows with the square of speed, so a rotor that is quiet at 1000 rpm can shake hard at 3000 rpm. The force is carried into the bearings, the casing and the foundation. The cost is bearing wear, seal damage, fatigue cracks, noise and lost efficiency.

  • Signature: unbalance shows up as vibration at exactly 1× running speed, steady in amplitude and phase.
  • Balancing means adding (or removing) a small mass at a chosen angle so its force cancels the existing one.
  • Check first: misalignment, looseness, bent shafts and resonance can also give 1× vibration. Balancing cannot fix those.
2Key termsAmplitude, phase, high spot, heavy spot
Amplitude
How big the 1× vibration is (mm/s, µm or g). Called magnitude in vector maths.
Phase angle
The angle between a reference mark on the shaft and the moment the sensor sees its peak. Amplitude and phase together form one vector.
High spot
The angular position of the rotor when the vibration peaks, as seen by the sensor.
Heavy spot
Where the unbalanced mass really is. It is not the same as the high spot: the machine responds with a delay (phase lag) that depends on speed and stiffness.
Trial weight
A known mass added at a known angle, to learn how this machine responds.
Correction weight
The mass and angle that cancel the unbalance. Add it, or remove the equivalent mass at the opposite side.
Residual
The vibration left after balancing. The target is that it falls inside an acceptable zone.

In the app: Original run, Trial run and Final readings are vectors; the polar plot draws them so you can see the geometry.

3The influence coefficient (IC)The one number that links weight to vibration

The influence coefficient α answers a single question: if I add 1 gram at the reference angle, how does the vibration vector change? It is a complex number, so it has a size and an angle:

α = (O+T − O) / Wt = T / Wt

  • Size (for example 0.53 mm/s per g): how sensitive the machine is. High sensitivity means small weights do a lot.
  • Angle (for example 141°): the phase shift the machine adds between the weight and the vibration it causes. This is the lag between heavy spot and high spot.

How it is measured: run once and record O, stop and fix a known trial weight Wt at a marked angle, run again at the same speed and record O+T. The calculator subtracts the vectors and divides by Wt.

Then the correction is simple: the weight must produce an effect equal to −O, so

Wc = −O / α

Its size is |O| / |α|, and its angle is the angle of O, minus the angle of α, plus 180°.

Make the IC trustworthy

  • Choose a trial weight large enough to change the vibration by roughly 30% in amplitude or 30° in phase. Smaller changes drown in noise.
  • Not so large that the vibration becomes dangerous or the rotor leaves its linear range. A common starting point is a trial weight whose centrifugal force is about 5% of the rotor weight, i.e. mt·r·ω² ≈ 0.05 · M · g. The workbench's Suggested trial weight uses exactly this rule.
  • Same speed, same sensor position, same units and same load for all runs. Let the machine settle thermally before reading.
  • Read phase carefully; a 10° reading error becomes a 10° error in the correction angle.

In the app: the IC is calculated for you after the Trial run and shown with the results. It is the number to record if you may balance this machine again.

4Worked example, step by stepFollow the numbers with a pencil
  1. Original run. O = 4.0 mm/s at 40°.
  2. Trial run. Add Wt = 10 g at 0°. Reading O+T = 6.0 mm/s at 100°.
  3. Trial effect. T = (O+T) − O. In rectangular form: (−1.04, 5.91) − (3.06, 2.57) = (−4.11, 3.34), which is 5.29 mm/s at 140.9°.
  4. Influence coefficient. α = T / 10 g = 0.529 mm/s per g at 140.9°.
  5. Correction. Size = 4.0 / 0.529 = 7.56 g. Angle = 40 − 140.9 + 180 = 79.1°.
  6. Check. Predicted residual: O + α·Wc = 0. In real life it is small but not zero, because of noise and non-linearity. That is why you always do a final run.

Housekeeping: normally the trial weight is taken off before the correction weight is fitted. Note in your records which you did, so the next person can interpret the data.

5Single-shot balancingBalance in one run when the IC is already known

The trial run is the slow part: an extra start-up, an extra stop, an extra weight to fit. If you already know α for this machine, you can skip it. Measure O, compute Wc = −O / α, fit it, and verify. That is single-shot (single-run, known-IC) balancing.

It works well when

  • It is the same machine (or a series of identical machines) and the same plane.
  • The speed is the same as when α was measured.
  • Sensor type, location and direction are unchanged.
  • Bearings, foundation and coupling are in similar condition.
  • The rotor mass has not changed (no new blades, coating or fouling).

Be careful when

  • The speed is near a critical speed or resonance, where α changes fast.
  • The machine was overhauled, re-supported or re-aligned.
  • Operating temperature differs a lot.
  • The IC came from a different machine and is only an estimate.

Always verify. After fitting the weight, run again. If the residual is acceptable you are done, and you have also learned a fresh IC. If not, treat the run as a trial run (see Trim balancing) and update α.

In the app: open the Known IC tab, enter the original readings and the IC, and calculate. Suggest Best IC Source helps you pick which earlier IC to trust. Save your jobs so the next one starts with an IC already in hand.

6Beyond one plane, one pointLeast squares, trim, splitting, two- and multi-plane
Several measuring locations
With one correction plane but more than one sensor, each location gives its own equation. The calculator finds the single weight that makes the total residual as small as possible (least squares), rather than trusting one location.
Trim balancing
A small final adjustment after the main correction, using the IC you already have. Typically the quickest way to polish the last few percent.
Weight splitting
The ideal angle may fall where you cannot weld or bolt. Split the weight into two components at neighbouring positions (for example, two blades) whose vector sum equals the required weight.
Two-plane (dynamic) balancing
Long rotors can be unbalanced in two planes at once, and each plane affects both bearings. You need a 2×2 matrix of influence coefficients, including the cross effects, and the maths becomes a pair of complex equations.
Multi-plane
The same idea with more planes and more sensors, solved as a larger complex system. Flexible rotors running through critical speeds need this.

In the app: see the Trim, Splitting, Two-Plane and Multi-Plane tabs for each of these.

Rule of thumb: disc-shaped rotors (fans, pulleys, flywheels) with length less than about a quarter of the diameter can usually be balanced in a single plane. Longer rotors usually need two.

7How good is good enough?ISO 20816-1 zones and ISO 21940 grades

Vibration zones (ISO 20816-1). Measured vibration is classed into four zones, with limits that depend on machine type and size:

ANewly commissioned machines normally fall here.
BAcceptable for unrestricted long-term operation.
CNot suitable for long-term operation. Plan correction.
DSevere enough to cause damage. Act now.

Balance quality grade (ISO 21940). A grade G (for example G2.5 or G6.3) fixes the permissible residual unbalance per unit of rotor mass, as a speed-independent quantity:

eper = G / ω    Uper = eper · m

Example: grade G6.3 at 3000 rpm (ω = 314 rad/s) gives eper = 6.3 / 314 = 20 µm. A 100 kg rotor may then have a residual unbalance of about 2005 g·mm. Higher speed demands a smaller allowed eccentricity.

In the app: zone badges colour the results (A green, B blue, C amber, D red). The advanced analytics panel has a Target grade selector (default G6.3) and estimates the residual against it using ISO 21940-11.

8Common pitfallsWhat usually goes wrong in the field
  • Wrong rotation convention. Measure angles in the same direction (with or against rotation) for the phase and for the weight. The CW/CCW switch exists for this.
  • Unstable readings. Average several readings; a wandering phase means resonance, looseness or a poor reference signal.
  • Trial weight left on. Decide in advance whether it stays or comes off.
  • Mixing units. mm/s, µm and g are not interchangeable between runs.
  • Mixing speeds. An IC from 1480 rpm is not valid at 1750 rpm.
  • Balancing a different problem. If the 1× phase changes between measuring points by 0–180°, or 2× is strong, look for misalignment or looseness first.
  • Skipping the final run. The prediction is not a measurement.
9GlossaryQuick definitions
ω
Angular speed in rad/s: 2π × rpm / 60.
g·mm
Unit of unbalance: mass in grams times radius in millimetres.
Vector method
Balancing using amplitude and phase, treated as complex numbers.
Phase lag
The angle by which vibration trails the force that causes it.
Linearity
The assumption that doubling the weight doubles the effect. Basis of the IC.
Tachometer / reference mark
The once-per-turn pulse that gives phase its zero.
Critical speed
A speed where a rotor naturally resonates. Avoid balancing exactly on it.
Static / couple unbalance
Static is a single heavy spot; couple is two equal heavy spots opposite and offset along the axis. Real rotors have both (dynamic unbalance).
